ServiceNow ITSM in Real Work: Incident, Request, Problem and Change Scenarios

A scenario-based guide to understanding everyday service-management workflows and how beginners can practise them.

When learning ServiceNow IT Service Management (ITSM), it is easy to memorise terms such as incident, request, problem and change without knowing when each one should be used. In a real support environment, choosing the right process helps teams route work, communicate with users and keep service operations organised.

This guide explains the four concepts through realistic examples. The scenarios are simplified for learning: organisations may use different policies, catalogue items, approval paths and service-level targets, so always follow the process defined by the relevant team.

Quick distinction: an incident is about an interruption or degradation; a request is for a standard service or item; a problem investigates underlying causes; and a change controls a planned modification to a service or environment.

1. Incident management: restore a service that is not working

Scenario: Several employees report that they cannot connect to a business application. One employee creates a ticket saying the page keeps timing out.

The immediate objective is to record the impact, understand the symptoms, route the ticket to the appropriate team and restore service as quickly and safely as possible. The support agent should not assume the cause from the first description.

What a learner should check

  • Capture a clear short description and relevant details, including when the issue began and which service is affected.
  • Assess impact and urgency according to the organisation's priority matrix rather than choosing a priority arbitrarily.
  • Check for related incidents, known service interruptions or existing guidance before repeating troubleshooting.
  • Assign the incident using the organisation's routing process and keep user-facing updates understandable.
  • Record the resolution and verify whether the service is working again before closing the ticket according to policy.

Practice exercise: create fictional tickets for one user unable to sign in and for a wider application outage. Explain why the two cases may have different impact and urgency. Do not treat a high priority as a substitute for gathering facts.

2. Service requests: fulfil a standard, approved need

Scenario: A new employee needs access to an approved application and a standard headset for their role.

This is generally a service request rather than an incident because the employee is asking for a standard service or item, not reporting that an existing service has unexpectedly failed. Depending on the organisation, requests may be submitted through a service catalogue and can involve eligibility checks, approvals, fulfilment tasks and status updates.

What a learner should check

  • Identify the exact catalogue item or standard request process.
  • Confirm that required information is supplied, such as the user's department or business justification where policy requires it.
  • Understand whether approval is needed and which authorised person or group provides it.
  • Track fulfilment separately from approval: an approved request may still need work by a fulfilment team.
  • Communicate what happens next and close the request only when the defined fulfilment process is complete.

Practice exercise: map a fictional software-access request from submission to approval and fulfilment. List the information needed at each stage, and note which steps depend on local policy.

3. Problem management: investigate recurring incidents

Scenario: Users have reported intermittent printing failures several times over two weeks. Each incident was resolved, but similar tickets continue to appear.

Incident management focuses on restoring service in an individual case. Problem management looks for the underlying cause behind one or more incidents and considers ways to prevent recurrence. A workaround may reduce disruption while investigation continues, but it is not necessarily a permanent fix.

What a learner should check

  • Look for patterns across related incidents, such as timing, location, affected devices or recent changes.
  • Record the known symptoms, suspected cause and evidence separately so assumptions are not presented as facts.
  • Document any workaround and explain its limitations.
  • Track investigation tasks and link related records when the organisation's process supports it.
  • After a corrective action, monitor whether the same issue returns and update relevant knowledge or support notes.

Practice exercise: write a short problem record for recurring fictional printing failures. Include three possible causes, what evidence would distinguish them and one safe test for each. The goal is to show structured reasoning, not guess the answer immediately.

4. Change management: make a planned modification in a controlled way

Scenario: An IT team plans to update a shared application outside normal working hours. The update could affect users if it fails.

A change process helps the organisation understand the purpose, risk, impact, schedule, approvals, implementation steps and back-out approach before a modification is made. The required controls vary by change type and local governance; not every change follows an identical approval route.

What a learner should check

  • Describe the proposed change, its business reason and the services or users that could be affected.
  • Identify risks, dependencies, validation steps and a practical back-out plan.
  • Follow the applicable assessment, authorisation and scheduling process.
  • Record implementation and validation results, including what happened if the change did not go as planned.
  • Complete the review and closure steps required by the organisation.

Practice exercise: draft a change plan for a fictional low-risk configuration update. Write the implementation steps, success criteria, likely risks and the action you would take if validation failed. Do not perform changes on production systems without explicit authorisation.

5. Compare the four workflows at a glance

Record typeMain questionExamplePrimary focus
IncidentWhat service is interrupted or degraded?An employee cannot access an application that normally works.Restore service and communicate progress.
Service requestWhat standard service or item is being requested?A user requests approved software access.Validate and fulfil the request.
ProblemWhy do incidents keep occurring?Similar application failures happen repeatedly.Investigate causes and reduce recurrence.
ChangeHow will a planned modification be controlled?A team schedules an application update.Assess, authorise, implement and review safely.

These records can be related, but they are not interchangeable. For example, a recurring incident may lead to a problem investigation, and a permanent corrective action may require a change record. The exact relationships depend on the organisation's process and configuration.

6. A simple way to practise these scenarios in ServiceNow

Use an authorised personal or training instance and fictional records. If your environment does not include every workflow, document the process on paper rather than assuming a feature is available.

  1. Write the user story. Describe who needs help, what happened and what outcome is expected.
  2. Choose the record type. Explain why it is an incident, request, problem or change.
  3. List the required information. Identify the fields, decisions, approvals or evidence needed to move the work forward.
  4. Define test cases. Include a normal case, missing information and one exception.
  5. Review the result. Compare expected and actual behaviour, note any gap and explain how you would correct it safely.

Keep a short learning log for each exercise. Record the scenario, your decision, the reasoning behind it and what you would verify in a real environment. This gives you more useful interview examples than memorising a list of definitions.

Frequently asked questions

What is the difference between an incident and a service request?

An incident usually describes an unplanned interruption or reduction in service quality. A service request asks for a standard service, information or approved item, such as access to software listed in a catalogue.

Is a problem the same as an incident?

No. An incident focuses on restoring service for the affected user, while problem management investigates underlying causes of one or more incidents and works to reduce recurrence.

Why is change management important?

It helps teams assess and control the risk of planned modifications through appropriate review, authorisation, scheduling, implementation and validation.

How should beginners practise ITSM?

Use an authorised learning environment with fictional data, work through realistic scenarios, test expected outcomes and document your reasoning. Never share credentials or confidential employer or customer information.

Final takeaway

Learning ITSM becomes more meaningful when you can decide which process fits a situation and explain what should happen next. Practise one scenario at a time, follow the rules of your learning environment and focus on the reasoning behind each step. These fundamentals provide a useful base for exploring more advanced ServiceNow workflows.
