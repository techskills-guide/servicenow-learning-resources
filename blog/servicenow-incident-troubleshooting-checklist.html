Why Do ServiceNow Incidents Get Stuck? 7 Troubleshooting Checks for Beginners

A diagnostic walkthrough for learners who want to understand unexpected incident behaviour instead of guessing at a fix.

You update an incident, click Save, and expect the next step to happen—but the state does not change, the assignment group remains empty, or an automation appears to do nothing. These symptoms can look similar, but they do not necessarily share the same cause.

The useful troubleshooting habit is to separate what you observed from what you suspect. First reproduce the issue in an authorised non-production environment, note the exact record state and action, and then test one possible cause at a time. ServiceNow instances differ in configuration, custom scripts, access rules and process design, so use the checks below as a diagnostic sequence rather than a universal list of fixes.

Before you begin: use a personal or training instance with fictional data. Do not disable business rules, change access controls, or edit production records just to see whether a symptom disappears. Follow your organisation's change and access policies.

1. Confirm the exact symptom and reproduce it

“The incident is stuck” is too broad to diagnose. Identify the specific behaviour: does the form reject a save, does the state revert after saving, does assignment fail, or does the record save while a related task never starts?

  • Record the incident number in your authorised practice environment, the current state and the state you expected.
  • Note the action immediately before the problem, such as selecting a state, changing a field or submitting the form.
  • Check whether the behaviour occurs every time or only under particular conditions.
  • Compare with a new fictional test record that has similar values, if it is safe and appropriate to do so.

Example: if an incident remains in “In Progress” after you choose “Resolved,” first confirm whether the save completed and whether the displayed value changes after a refresh. That observation helps distinguish a save or validation issue from a later automation or display issue.

2. Check mandatory fields and state-transition requirements

A record may not progress if a required field is empty or if the configured process requires information before a particular transition. Resolution details, closure codes or other fields may be mandatory depending on the instance and its configuration.

What to check:

  • Read any on-screen validation message carefully instead of repeatedly clicking Save.
  • Look for highlighted fields or required-field indicators.
  • Check whether the target state has process-specific requirements in your learning environment.
  • Test a valid example and an intentionally incomplete example in a safe practice instance, then compare the results.

Diagnostic question: does the form save successfully when the required information is supplied? If yes, document which field or condition mattered. If no, continue investigating rather than assuming the mandatory-field check explains everything.

3. Investigate assignment rules and group conditions

If an incident is not routed to the expected assignment group, inspect the conditions that should trigger assignment. A rule may depend on category, subcategory, service, location or other fields. The record might not meet those conditions, or another rule or automation might update the assignment later.

  • Verify that the values on the test record match the rule's conditions exactly.
  • Check whether the intended assignment group exists and is eligible for the configured process.
  • Review rule order and any competing assignment logic where you have permission to do so.
  • Check whether a later update changes the group after the initial assignment.

Example: suppose a routing rule assigns network incidents to the Network Support group, but your test ticket stays unassigned. Confirm that the category and any additional conditions actually match the rule. Do not immediately create another rule; duplicate or overlapping rules can make behaviour harder to understand.

4. Check business rules and client-side behaviour

Business rules and client scripts can influence record values and form behaviour. A script might set a field, prevent an action under a condition or change a value during a save. Customisations can also conflict with one another.

Begin with the smallest useful evidence:

  • Reproduce the issue and write down the exact field values before and after the action.
  • Look for visible error messages and relevant diagnostic output available to your role.
  • With appropriate access, inspect applicable business rules or client scripts and their conditions.
  • Check whether the behaviour is limited to one form, one role or a particular combination of field values.

Do not disable a script just because its name looks relevant. In a controlled practice environment, test a specific hypothesis and preserve the original configuration so you can restore it. On a shared or production instance, follow the authorised review and change process.

5. Verify Flow Designer or other automation actually ran

Sometimes the incident record saves correctly but the expected follow-up action does not occur—for example, a notification, task or approval is not created. In that case, distinguish a record-update problem from an automation-execution problem.

  • Confirm the automation is intended to run for this record and this event.
  • Check the trigger conditions and whether the changed field or state meets them.
  • Use the execution details or available flow context to see whether it ran, was skipped or encountered an error.
  • Check whether a failed action depends on missing input, unavailable access or a referenced record that does not exist.
  • Verify that the expected result is part of the flow's actual design; not every state change triggers a separate workflow.

Example: a flow designed to run when an incident enters a particular state may not start if the trigger condition checks a different state value or requires an additional field. Compare the record's actual values with the configured trigger before editing the flow.

6. Consider roles, permissions and record access

Two users can experience different behaviour if their roles and access differ. A field may be read-only, a related record may be inaccessible, or a scripted action may depend on permissions. Avoid treating a permissions issue as a reason to grant broad access.

  • Check whether the field is editable for the current user and role.
  • Compare the behaviour with an authorised test account or role, where permitted.
  • Review relevant access-control conditions only if your role allows it.
  • Ask an administrator to validate permissions when the evidence points to access controls.

Use the principle of least privilege: the goal is to identify the required permission or condition, not to give a learner or user administrator access as a shortcut.

7. Review evidence, timing and recent changes

If the cause is still unclear, assemble the evidence before changing anything. Depending on your access and the instance configuration, useful sources may include record history, audit information, system logs, flow execution details and recent configuration changes.

  • Write a short timeline: action taken, time, observed result and any error.
  • Check whether the problem began after a recent configuration change or release.
  • Compare a working test case with a failing one and identify the first meaningful difference.
  • Repeat the test after changing only one variable, then record the result.
  • Escalate with the reproduction steps and evidence if you cannot safely inspect the relevant configuration.

A concise diagnostic note is more useful than “it does not work.” For example: “In the training instance, a fictional incident saves, but the assignment group remains blank when category is set to Network. A test record with category Hardware assigns correctly. No visible validation error appears.” This gives the next person a clear starting point without claiming a cause that has not been proven.

Practice exercise: investigate a fictional incident

Imagine a test incident with category “Network.” After saving, the incident remains in its original state and the expected assignment group is not populated. You do not know whether the two symptoms share a cause.

  1. Reproduce the issue and note whether the save succeeds.
  2. Check required fields and the exact state transition being attempted.
  3. Compare the incident values with the assignment rule conditions.
  4. Review relevant execution or diagnostic details that you are authorised to access.
  5. Change one variable at a time in the training environment and record whether the result changes.
  6. Write a brief conclusion that separates confirmed facts from remaining hypotheses.

The exercise is complete when you can explain the evidence and the next safe test—not merely when the record behaves differently.

Frequently asked questions

Why might a ServiceNow incident not move to the next state?

Possible reasons include missing required fields, configured transition requirements, scripts, permissions or automation. Check the exact behaviour and evidence from your instance before deciding which explanation fits.

Why is an incident not assigned to the expected group?

Review the assignment conditions, record values, rule order and any later automation that may change the assignment. Confirm the group and user meet the requirements of the configured process.

Should I disable a business rule to troubleshoot?

Not as a first step. Identify a specific hypothesis and use an authorised non-production environment. On shared or production systems, follow the approved review and change process.

Can beginners practise troubleshooting safely?

Yes. Use an authorised personal or training instance, fictional records and controlled tests. Avoid exposing confidential information or changing production configuration without permission.

Final takeaway

When an incident appears stuck, avoid random edits. Define the symptom, reproduce it safely, check the relevant conditions and automation, review evidence, and test one hypothesis at a time. This approach builds transferable troubleshooting habits and makes it easier to explain what you found to another administrator or support team.
